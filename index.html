My scholarly work examines environmental activism, visual rhetoric, and digital media, with particular emphasis on how social movements mobilize public attention through image events and networked communication. Over the past two decades, I have developed an analytical framework centered on what I call "wild public screens"—spaces where activism encounters censorship, surveillance, and the contingencies of digital platforms. My recent research explores environmental movements in China, where I investigate how activists navigate state control to articulate alternative visions of ecological futures.

I teach courses in media theory, visual rhetoric, environmental communication, and the politics of technology. I am interested in how communication shapes our relationship to nature, how images generate political force, and how digital media enable—and constrain—forms of collective action.
